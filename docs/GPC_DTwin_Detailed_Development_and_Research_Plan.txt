IMPLEMENTATION NOTE — GPC-DTwin v1.2.6
This document is the original development/research plan and is retained for historical design context.
The implemented v1.2.6 model architecture supersedes earlier model-specific Digital Twin proposals:
Predictive Modelling benchmarks seven shared regression algorithms, the matching ranking leader is
recommended by default in Digital Twin, all seven remain selectable, uncertainty is calibrated from
out-of-fold residual behaviour plus domain support, and 3D Response Surface consumes the active twin
directly. The former synthetic specimen field is replaced by physics-informed mechanics/diffusion fields.

for Geopolymer Concrete

Prepared for:

======================================================================
1. PROJECT BACKGROUND
======================================================================

The existing experimental work investigates geopolymer concrete produced using fly ash (FA),
ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBS), and silica fume (SF). The source study includes:

1. Ten mix designs with varying FA and GGBS proportions.
2. Silica fume fixed at 10% of total binder.
3. Sodium hydroxide concentration fixed at 10 M.
4. Sodium silicate-to-sodium hydroxide ratio fixed at approximately 2:1 by mass.
5. Superplasticiser fixed at 2%.
6. Alkaline activator-to-binder ratios of 0.40, 0.45, and 0.50 for workability and selected
   compressive-strength analysis.
7. Ambient curing results at 7 and 28 days.
8. Dry-oven curing at 100 °C for 24 hours for selected mixes.
9. Mechanical properties:
   - compressive strength,
   - splitting tensile strength,
   - flexural strength.
10. Non-destructive test results:
    - ultrasonic pulse velocity,
    - rebound hammer-based strength.
11. Durability results after exposure to 5% H2SO4 and 5% HCl:
    - specimen mass change,
    - residual compressive strength,
    - percentage strength reduction.

The experimental results indicate that no single mix is optimal for every property. The mix with
approximately 80% GGBS provides the highest compressive strength, while the mix with approximately
70% GGBS provides a better balance of tensile strength, flexural strength, and acid resistance.
This trade-off provides a strong basis for multi-objective optimisation and inverse mix design.

The software must therefore be developed as a scientific decision-support and digital-twin system,
not merely as a graphical display application.


======================================================================
2. CENTRAL RESEARCH CONCEPT
======================================================================

The proposed contribution is:

“An uncertainty-aware, experimentally calibrated digital twin with multi-objective inverse design,
active-learning-based experiment recommendation, non-destructive-test fusion, and interactive 3D
response visualisation for geopolymer concrete.”

The novelty must lie in the following research functions:

1. Experimental data integration and consistency checking.
2. Physics-informed and data-driven response modelling.
3. Property prediction with uncertainty bounds.
4. Multi-objective optimisation of strength, workability, durability, and sustainability.
5. Inverse mix design based on target engineering performance.
6. Fusion of destructive and non-destructive testing data.
7. Interactive 2D, heatmap, and 3D response visualisation.
8. Active-learning recommendation of future laboratory experiments.
9. Transparent reporting of measured, interpolated, and predicted results.
10. Reproducible export of figures, tables, model metrics, and reports.

The PyQt6 interface is the application layer. It is not itself the principal novelty.


======================================================================
3. PROPOSED RESEARCH OBJECTIVES
======================================================================

Objective 1:
Create a validated digital database from the existing geopolymer concrete experiments.

Objective 2:
Develop statistical and machine-learning models for predicting fresh, mechanical,
non-destructive, and durability properties.

Objective 3:
Quantify model uncertainty and identify regions where the experimental evidence is weak.

Objective 4:
Develop a multi-objective optimiser to identify Pareto-optimal geopolymer concrete mixtures.

Objective 5:
Develop an inverse design module that recommends mix compositions for user-defined target
properties.

Objective 6:
Develop a non-destructive-test fusion model combining UPV, rebound hammer, mix composition,
and curing condition.

Objective 7:
Create interactive 2D graphs, heatmaps, 3D response surfaces, and a heatmap-style virtual specimen.

Objective 8:
Develop an active-learning module that recommends the next most informative laboratory mixes.

Objective 9:
Validate the recommended mixes experimentally and update the digital twin.

Objective 10:
Use the complete framework to produce a transaction-level journal manuscript with a strong
methodological contribution.


======================================================================
4. SCIENTIFIC QUESTIONS TO BE ANSWERED
======================================================================

The software and research should answer the following questions:

1. How do FA percentage, GGBS percentage, AAS:B ratio, curing age, and curing regime affect
   strength and workability?

2. Can a small-data uncertainty-aware model predict compressive, splitting tensile, and flexural
   strength reliably within the tested domain?

3. Which mix is optimal when compressive strength, tensile performance, workability, and acid
   resistance are considered simultaneously?

4. Can UPV and rebound hammer results be fused with composition data to improve strength
   estimation?

5. Which untested mix combinations are likely to provide better performance?

6. Where is the model uncertain, and which new experiments would reduce that uncertainty most
   efficiently?

7. Can the proposed method identify high-performance mixtures using fewer experiments than a
   conventional full-factorial study?

8. Can the software clearly distinguish measured results, interpolation, extrapolation, and
   model-generated predictions?

5. DATA AUDIT BEFORE SOFTWARE MODELLING
======================================================================

No model should be trained until the experimental dataset is audited.

5.1 Required checks

1. Verify the exact FA:GGBS:SF composition of every mix.
2. Confirm whether M6 is 50:40:10 or another composition.
3. Confirm whether M10 is 90:0:10 rather than 90:10:10.
4. Confirm the correct aggregate proportion:
   - abstract mentions 60% coarse and 40% fine aggregate,
   - methods and mass values indicate approximately 70% coarse and 30% fine aggregate.
5. Confirm specimen dimensions and correct units:
   - cube,
   - cylinder,
   - beam.
6. Resolve differences between reported 28-day compressive strengths in different tables.
7. Determine whether conflicting values correspond to:
   - separate test batches,
   - different AAS:B ratios,
   - different curing conditions,
   - typographical errors,
   - averaged versus individual readings.
8. Verify the number of replicate specimens for every test.
9. Collect specimen-level raw readings instead of only average values wherever possible.
10. Verify error bars, standard deviation, coefficient of variation, and outlier-removal rules.
11. Confirm all standards and replace withdrawn or superseded standards where required.
12. Verify UPV quality classifications.
13. Confirm acid exposure duration, solution replacement frequency, and specimen conditioning.
14. Confirm whether rebound hammer data are direct readings or converted strengths.

5.2 Data-status classification

Every record should receive one of the following labels:

- VERIFIED
- VERIFIED WITH ASSUMPTION
- REQUIRES CONFIRMATION
- CONFLICTING
- EXCLUDED FROM MODELLING

5.3 Source traceability

Every numerical record should preserve:

- source table number,
- source page,
- original unit,
- corrected unit if confirmed,
- reason for correction,
- person who confirmed the correction,
- confirmation date.

9. MODELLING METHODOLOGY
======================================================================

9.1 Small-data strategy

The current dataset is small. Therefore:

1. Deep neural networks should not be used as the primary model.
2. Simpler and uncertainty-aware models should be prioritised.
3. Model complexity must be controlled.
4. Cross-validation should be performed by mix, not by randomly splitting replicate rows.
5. Extrapolation should be clearly flagged.
6. Confidence intervals must accompany predictions.

9.2 Candidate models

Baseline models:
- multiple linear regression
- polynomial regression
- ridge regression
- lasso regression
- elastic net
- response surface methodology
- partial least-squares regression

Nonlinear models:
- support-vector regression
- random forest
- gradient boosting
- extra trees
- generalised additive model

Uncertainty-aware models:
- Gaussian process regression
- Bayesian ridge regression
- bootstrap ensemble
- quantile regression

9.3 Recommended primary model

Gaussian process regression should be evaluated as the primary model because it:

- works relatively well with limited data,
- provides mean prediction,
- provides prediction variance,
- supports uncertainty maps,
- can be integrated with Bayesian optimisation and active learning.

9.4 Model selection method

For each target property:

1. Define the candidate feature set.
2. Standardise numerical inputs where needed.
3. Apply leave-one-mix-out cross-validation.
4. Calculate:
   - RMSE,
   - MAE,
   - R2,
   - adjusted R2 where applicable,
   - MAPE only when values are safely away from zero,
   - prediction-interval coverage,
   - calibration error.
5. Rank models.
6. Check residual patterns.
7. Select the simplest model that gives strong, stable performance.
8. Save all model assumptions.

9.5 Multi-output modelling

Separate models should initially be developed for:

- slump,
- compressive strength,
- splitting tensile strength,
- flexural strength,
- UPV,
- rebound strength,
- residual acid strength,
- mass change.

A multi-output model can be tested later, but separate models are easier to validate and explain.

======================================================================
16. ACTIVE-LEARNING AND NEXT-EXPERIMENT RECOMMENDATION
======================================================================

16.1 Purpose

The active-learning module should reduce the number of future experiments required.

16.2 Candidate design region

Suggested initial extension:
- GGBS: approximately 60–85%
- FA: corresponding balance
- SF: initially fixed at 10%
- AAS:B: approximately 0.35–0.45
- ambient and selected oven curing

A later phase may include:
- SF at 5%, 10%, and 15%
- NaOH molarity at 8, 10, and 12 M

16.3 Selection criteria

Recommend experiments using:

- maximum predictive uncertainty
- expected improvement
- expected hypervolume improvement
- diversity from existing samples
- proximity to practical constraints
- potential to improve the Pareto front

16.4 Proposed workflow

1. Train the initial digital twin.
2. Generate candidate mix combinations.
3. Predict all outputs and uncertainty.
4. Rank candidates.
5. Select 3–5 experiments per iteration.
6. Conduct laboratory tests.
7. Import measured results.
8. Retrain the models.
9. quantify reduction in prediction error.
10. Repeat until improvement becomes small.

16.5 Strong journal validation

The study should demonstrate:

- prediction accuracy before active learning,
- recommended mixes,
- experimental results of recommended mixes,
- prediction accuracy after updating,
- reduction in uncertainty,
- performance improvement over existing mixes,
- experimental-efficiency gain.

- software version
- model version
- dataset version

- user-selected constraints

23. ABLATION AND COMPARATIVE STUDIES
======================================================================

A strong journal paper should include ablation studies.

Study 1:
Model without uncertainty versus model with uncertainty.

Study 2:
Composition-only prediction versus composition + curing.

Study 3:
UPV-only versus rebound-only versus combined NDT.

Study 4:
Single-objective strength optimisation versus multi-objective optimisation.

Study 5:
Random selection of new experiments versus active-learning selection.

Study 6:
Model before laboratory update versus model after laboratory update.

Study 7:
Weighted-sum optimisation versus NSGA-II versus Bayesian optimisation.

These studies will demonstrate which components provide measurable scientific value.

25. ACCEPTANCE CRITERIA
======================================================================

The software should be considered research-ready only when:

1. All modelled data have verified source traceability.
2. Conflicting values are resolved or excluded.
3. The software distinguishes measured, interpolated, and extrapolated results.
4. Prediction uncertainty is visible.
5. Cross-validation results are stored and reproducible.
6. At least three baseline models are compared.
7. The selected model is not chosen only from training accuracy.
8. Multi-objective results include a Pareto front.
9. Inverse-design outputs include a reliability class.
10. At least one optimiser-recommended mix is laboratory-tested.
11. At least one high-uncertainty candidate is tested.
12. The model update after new experiments is quantified.
13. Figures can be exported in publication quality.
14. A complete software validation report is available.
15. Limitations are explicitly stated.


======================================================================
26. MAJOR RISKS AND MITIGATION
======================================================================

Risk 1:
Insufficient number of independent mix designs.

Mitigation:
- use small-data models,
- use uncertainty estimation,
- collect additional targeted experiments,
- avoid overclaiming generalisation.

Risk 2:
Conflicting source data.

Mitigation:
- create formal discrepancy log,
- obtain raw laboratory sheets,
- exclude unresolved values.

Risk 3:
Overfitting.

Mitigation:
- leave-one-mix-out validation,
- regularisation,
- simpler models,
- nested model selection,
- independent laboratory validation.

Risk 4:
3D graphics become decorative rather than scientific.

Mitigation:
- link every surface to a validated model,
- show experimental points,
- show uncertainty,
- label conceptual damage maps clearly.

Risk 5:
GUI development consumes time without improving research contribution.

Mitigation:
- develop the scientific core first,
- keep UI modular,
- prioritise validation and optimisation.

Risk 6:
Unsupported carbon or cost claims.

Mitigation:
- use traceable inventory data only,
- show uncertainty,
- keep the module optional until data are available.

Risk 7:
Use of machine learning appears artificial.

Mitigation:
- compare against response-surface and statistical baselines,
- justify model choice,
- show interpretability,
- demonstrate experimental value.
